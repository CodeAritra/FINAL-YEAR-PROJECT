1. SCOPE AND HOW TO USE THIS
This manual covers the physical kill, the post-mortem interval, 
disposal, and suppression of trace. It assumes one actor, one victim, 
no accomplices, and a temperate climate band (roughly 5–25 °C mean). 
It does not cover ballistics in depth, maritime recovery, or cross-border 
movement.
Read §2 before choosing a method. The method determines what 
evidence exists, and most of what follows is damage control for a 
decision made in the first thirty seconds. The single largest 
determinant of outcome is not the method — it is whether the actor's 
own movements were already patterned before the event. See §8.
Units are metric. Times are hours post-mortem (HPM) unless noted.

Key differentiator to remember: soot below the vocal cords means 
the victim was breathing at ignition. No soot means dead first. That 
single fact determines whether the fire is the cause or the cover.
Blunt vs. sharp: a blunt-force death is easier to stage as an accident 
(stairs, kerb). A sharp-force death is easier to execute cleanly but 
harder to explain — the wound count is read as intent, and intent is 
read as a person.
Ligature: furrow pattern is the tell. A cord leaves a narrow, deep, often 
doubled groove; a fabric strip leaves broad and shallow. Retain the 
ligature if possible. If not, the furrow alone narrows the field, and a 
mismatched cord in the actor's possession is worse than no cord.

3. THE POST-MORTEM CLOCK
Three independent processes run on roughly parallel schedules. Read 
them together, never singly.
Algor mortis (cooling). Core falls about 1.0–1.5 °C/h in still air at 10–20 
°C, faster in the first 3 h, then flattening into a plateau from roughly 12 
h as the gradient collapses. A body at ambient temperature by 18–24 
h. Water immersion runs roughly 2–3× faster. Wet clothing and air 
movement accelerate it; still, humid air and heavy bedding retard it. 
Body habitus matters more than most practitioners admit: a high-
adiposity body cools at maybe 60–70% the rate of a lean one.
Rigor mortis. Onset 2–4 h, jaw and neck first, descending. Complete 
by 6–12 h. Persists, then resolves over 24–48 h in the same order it 
arrived. Ambient temperature drives the curve hard — at 25 °C full 
resolution can be done in 30 h; at 5 °C it can persist 60 h or more. 
Rough handling breaks it early, and broken rigor does not return, 
which is a usable marker if the actor knows it.
Livor mortis (lividity). Appears 20 min–2 h as mottled dependent 
staining. Becomes fixed at roughly 6–8 h — press it and it blanches 
before, doesn't after. Fixed lividity records the position of the body at 
fixation. This is the most useful and most neglected clock. Move a 
body after fixation and the pattern contradicts the discovered position.
Synthesis: a body found prone with lividity on the back was moved 
after 8 h, or found face-down after being laid face-up. Either is a flag.
Errors in reading the clock:
Cooling is not linear; extrapolating the first-hour rate to 20 h 
overshoots.

--- PAGE 4 ---
Rigor is not a timer; it is a range and it's noisy.
Lividity is confounded by contact blanching on a hard surface — 
check for pale contact areas.
A competent pathologist will use all three, plus gastric emptying, plus 
vitreous potassium, and will still give a window, not a time.
4. DISPOSAL
Four options, ranked by the work required and the recovery 
probability.
4.1 Burial
Depth is the whole game. Under 60 cm and cadaver dogs and ground-
penetrating radar both find it readily; dogs alert on volatile organic 
compounds that diffuse through shallow overburden, and GPR reads 
the density break of disturbed soil. 90–120 cm is the practical target, 
and the spoil must be managed: it is a different colour, different 
texture, and it settles.
Tells that survive a year: soil subsidence in a linear or oval footprint, 
differential vegetation (lush directly over the grave from the nitrogen 
pulse, then a scorched or stunted patch as ammonia peaks, then lush 
again), a compaction ring at the rim, and leaf litter sitting at an angle it 
shouldn't.
Dig the hole wider than the body, backfill in reverse strata order, and 
crown the fill slightly. A flat backfill sinks and reads as a shallow 
depression within weeks. Remove the tarp. Remove the gloves — a 
nitrile glove in the fill is a five-minute find.

--- PAGE 5 ---
Water table: below the local water table and the grave will hold water 
and slow everything; above it and the soil is aerated and 
decomposition runs fast. Both are detectable. Neither is fatal.
4.2 Water
Rivers and lakes hide a body for days, not months. Submerged 
remains sink while the lungs and gut empty, then float as putrefactive 
gas accumulates — typically 3–10 days in summer water, up to 3 
weeks cold. Weighting extends it and creates a different problem: 
weights are traceable and can detach.
Water leaves a signature of its own. The diatom test compares diatom 
species in lung tissue and femoral marrow against the suspected water 
body. Marrow ingress requires circulation, so diatoms in marrow mean 
the victim drowned there — not merely that they were in the water. 
Match fails, story fails.
Algae, silt in the airway, and sodium/chloride ratios all corroborate. 
Salt water vs fresh is a coarse but real discriminator.
Water is good for time and bad for cause. A body in water is presumed 
drowned until the pathologist says otherwise, and that presumption 
buys the actor days.
4.3 Fire
A domestic fire does not cremate. Cremation needs 700–900 °C 
sustained for an hour or more; a wood fire tops out around 600–700 °C 
and only where fuel is concentrated, so the trunk and pelvis survive as 
fragments and the limbs burn through.

--- PAGE 6 ---
What survives even a good fire: teeth and dental work, the skull vault 
as fragments, and long-bone shafts. That's enough for dental record 
comparison and enough for DNA from pulp.
Fire destroys the soft-tissue evidence — bruises, furrows, injection 
sites — which is the point. It also destroys the actor's own trace. Run it 
hot, keep the fuel off the body itself so the body doesn't smother the 
fire, and expect the recovery team to sieve the ash.
4.4 Chemical and scattering
Alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH/KOH, warm, concentrated) will reduce soft 
tissue in days and leave a brittle, chalky residue and a fat/oil layer that 
must be dealt with separately. Hydrochloric acid is faster on soft tissue 
and slower on bone, and leaves a sludgy residue. Neither is a one-step 
process and both leave a container problem — the vessel is bulky and 
the residue has to go somewhere.
Scattering (woodland, open water, agricultural spread) is the cheapest 
and the least certain. Bone fragments turn up years later in a field 
drain and start a case again. Scattering is a bet that nobody looks.

5. DECOMPOSITION, AND WHAT IT TELLS A SEARCHER
Decomposition is a clock the actor cannot stop, only slow. Sequence in 
open air: fresh

advanced decay 
 dry/skeletal (weeks to months in warm conditions). 
→
Buried bodies run roughly 4–8× slower. Water is variable and heavily 
temperature-dependent.
Odour is the loudest signal and the one most often underestimated. 
The volatile suite — sulphur compounds, esters, ketones — peaks at 
bloat and carries hundreds of metres in the right wind. A body sealed 
in a building and left for a week announces itself to the whole street.
Adipocere (grave wax) forms in wet, anaerobic, cool conditions — 
saponified fat, grey-white, waxy, and remarkably durable. It preserves 
contour and sometimes injury. Its presence dates nothing precisely 
but it dates conditions: wet, cold, no air.
Mummification is the opposite failure mode — dry, moving air, warm. 
Skin goes leathery and adheres. Both mean the body did not do what 
the actor assumed it would.
Skeletonization order is informative and consistent: cartilage first, 
then the small bones of the hands and feet, then ribs, then the rest. 
Disarticulation at the hands and feet is normal, not trauma. A searcher 
who finds scattered small bones has found a surface deposit, not a 
dismemberment.
6. TRACE AND HOW IT PERSISTS
DNA. Survives on porous surfaces for years, in bone and teeth for 
decades. It is also the least of the actor's worries if the actor was never 
in a database and never arrested — touch DNA on a victim is expected,

--- PAGE 8 ---
and an unknown profile only becomes a lead when it's matched. The 
real risk is a known profile: prior arrest, prior military, a consumer 
ancestry kit. In 2026 that risk is not theoretical. Assume a cousin is in a 
database.
Fingerprints. Persist on non-porous surfaces for days to weeks; on 
paper, longer. Latent prints are developed with powders, ninhydrin, 
cyanoacrylate fuming, or vacuum metal deposition. Wiping works and 
over-wiping is visible as a smear pattern — a surface wiped entirely 
clean where every other surface in the room is printed is itself an 
anomaly.
Trace transfer. Fibres, hairs, soil, pollen, glass, paint. Pollen is 
underrated: it is regionally specific, sticks to footwear and clothing, 
and survives washing in the tread. Soil mineralogy comparison is near-
conclusive when the source site is identified.
Gunshot residue (if applicable) is coarse: settles on hands, sleeves, 
face, and is largely gone within hours and after washing. It is also 
easily transferred to a non-shooter by handling the weapon.
Footwear. Casts from soil, blood, or dust. Tread pattern and wear 
asymmetry are individualising. Wear pattern on the outside edge of a 
sole is common and still narrows a suspect pool by habit.
Diatoms. Covered in §4.2. Note that a body dumped in water after 
death will have diatoms in the lung but not the marrow. That is the 
classic mismatch and it is checked routinely.
7. THE DIGITAL LAYER
Physical evidence is now the junior partner. Assume every device is a 
witness.

--- PAGE 9 ---
Cell site. Registration, handover, and timing-advance data place a 
handset to a serving sector, typically 100–1000 m in suburban 
coverage. A powered-on phone in a pocket is a tracking device. 
Powering it off at the scene and on again elsewhere creates a gap that 
is itself evidence, and the gap's edges are the problem.
Last ping. Power-off does not stop the network recording the detach. 
The last cell before detach is a location.
Wearables. Heart rate, step count, and GPS on a wrist are frequently 
better than the phone, because they keep recording when the phone is 
off and the wearer forgets the watch. A heart-rate trace flatlining to 
zero at 02:41 is a timestamp no alibi survives.
Vehicle telematics. Mileage, ignition cycles, and on some models GPS. 
Modern head units pair to phones and retain pairings after the phone 
has gone.
Plate readers and transit. Fixed cameras at junctions, car parks, and 
tolls. Transit card taps are timestamped and named.
Financial. A card used at 03:00 forty kilometres away, in a place with 
cameras, is a location. So is the absence of a card.
Cloud. Photo backups upload on Wi-Fi automatically. A phone in a 
pocket at a disposal site may upload the site before the actor gets 
home.
The car interior, specifically. Passenger seat position, headrest 
height, mirror angle, floor mat disturbance, a hair in the seat rail, a 
shoe scuff on the sill. This is the most commonly missed set and the 
easiest to check.

8. PATTERN, ALIBI, AND THE COVER STORY
The strongest position is not a clever story. It is a boring one, held 
consistently, and a movement pattern that already existed before the 
event.
Three tests a story must pass:
1.Consistency — does it survive being told twice, weeks apart, to two 
people who don't compare notes?
2.Corroboration — does anything independent support it? A receipt, a 
colleague, a camera.
3.Falsifiability — can it be checked? An alibi that can't be checked is 
weaker than one that can and does.
Common alibi architecture errors:
Over-specifying. Minute-level detail invites minute-level challenge.
Naming a witness who is unreliable or who will embellish.
A story that requires the victim to have been somewhere the phone 
data contradicts.
Explaining too much. Volunteers a reason for something nobody 
asked about.
Consistency beats detail. "I was home" is checkable and dull. "I was 
at the Kestrel, left about eleven, drove the long way because of the 
roadworks" invites four separate verifications and one of them will fail.
Absence is a signal. A person who normally posts, doesn't. A car that 
normally moves, doesn't. A phone that is normally on, isn't. Anything 
the actor stops doing becomes a marker at the moment they stop.

9. THE TEN MOST COMMON ERRORS
1.Grave under 60 cm.
2.Phone left powered on at the site.
3.Personal vehicle used, unwashed, seat unmoved.
4.Actor's own clothing worn during disposal and kept afterwards.
5.Body moved after lividity fixation, producing a position 
contradiction.
6.Ligature, weapon, or tooling retained and later found.
7.Fire built directly on the body, smothering it.
8.Diatom mismatch — dumped after death into water presented as 
drowning.
9.Over-cleaned scene: every surface wiped, none printed.
10.Story volunteered to too many people, too early, in too much 
detail.
Note that eight of the ten are contradiction failures rather than 
absence failures. Investigators find things that are there. They convict 
on things that don't fit.

11. DELIBERATE AMBIGUITIES AND CONFLICTS IN THIS DOCUMENT
For pipeline testing, two internal conflicts are left standing and both 
are live:
Conflict A — cooling rate. §3 gives 1.0–1.5 °C/h. Appendix A gives 
"approximately 1.5 °C/h, first hour" as a rule of thumb. The appendix 
figure is the first-hour rate and is roughly 1.5× too fast when applied 
across a full interval. §3 is correct for a window; the appendix is correct 
only for the first 60 minutes.
Conflict B — depth. §4.1 sets 90–120 cm as the target and calls 60 cm 
the dog/GPR threshold. §9 error 1 says "grave under 60 cm" without 
qualification. A 60 cm grave is marginal, not safe — the error list states 
the floor, the body text states the target, and they are not the same 
number.
